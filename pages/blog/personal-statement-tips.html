Writing a Personal Statement That Stands Out

9 min read

Your personal statement is your chance to show admissions tutors why you're genuinely passionate about your chosen subject — and why you'd thrive on their course. Here's how to write one that stands out.

What admissions tutors actually look for

Forget the myths about needing a dramatic opening or a unique life story. Admissions tutors are looking for three things:

  1. Academic passion: Evidence that you're genuinely interested in the subject beyond the classroom
  2. Intellectual curiosity: That you've explored topics independently — reading, projects, competitions
  3. Reflection: That you can think critically about what you've learned and articulate why it matters to you

Structure that works

A personal statement has a 4,000-character limit (about 600 words). Every sentence needs to earn its place. Here's a structure that works well:

Opening (10%)

Start with a specific moment, question, or idea that sparked your interest. Avoid cliches like "I've always been passionate about..." — show, don't tell.

Academic exploration (60%)

This is the core of your statement. Discuss what you've done to explore your subject beyond the syllabus:

  • Books, articles, or papers you've read — and what you learned or questioned
  • Projects, competitions, or online courses
  • Relevant work experience or volunteering
  • Topics within the subject that fascinate you and why

Skills and extracurriculars (20%)

Briefly mention activities that demonstrate relevant skills — leadership, teamwork, communication. Link them back to how they'll help you succeed at university.

Conclusion (10%)

End by bringing it together: what you want to gain from the course and what you'll bring to it. Keep it forward-looking and specific.

The difference between a good and great personal statement is reflection. Don't just list what you've done — explain what you thought about it, what questions it raised, and how it deepened your understanding.

Common mistakes

  • Being too generic: "Economics is relevant to everyday life" tells the reader nothing. Be specific about what interests you and why.
  • Listing without reflecting: A list of books you've read without any analysis is just a bibliography.
  • Spending too long on extracurriculars: Duke of Edinburgh and sports are fine to mention, but the majority of your statement should be about your subject.
  • Trying to be funny or shocking: Admissions tutors read thousands of these. Substance beats style every time.

The editing process

Your first draft will not be your final draft. Plan to write at least 3-4 versions. After each draft:

  1. Read it aloud — does it sound like you?
  2. Ask a teacher to review it for subject-specific feedback
  3. Cut anything that doesn't directly serve your argument
  4. Check: could someone else have written this statement? If yes, make it more specific to you.
