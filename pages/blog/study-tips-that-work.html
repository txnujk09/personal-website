Study Techniques That Actually Work

8 min read

Most students spend hours revising inefficiently — re-reading notes, highlighting textbooks, copying out information. These methods feel productive but barely help with long-term retention. Here are the techniques that actually work, backed by research.

1. Active Recall

Instead of reading your notes, close them and try to recall the information from memory. This forces your brain to retrieve information, which strengthens the neural pathways associated with that knowledge.

How to do it:

  • After reading a topic, close your notes and write down everything you remember
  • Use flashcards (Anki is excellent for this)
  • Do practice questions without looking at your notes first
  • Teach the concept to someone else — or explain it to an empty room

2. Spaced Repetition

Instead of cramming everything the night before, spread your revision over days and weeks. Review material at increasing intervals: after 1 day, then 3 days, then 7 days, then 14 days. Each time you successfully recall something at a longer interval, the memory becomes more durable.

Cramming lets you pass tomorrow's test. Spaced repetition lets you remember the material for the exam — and beyond.

3. The Pomodoro Technique

Work in focused 25-minute blocks with 5-minute breaks. After four blocks, take a longer 15-30 minute break. This works because:

  • 25 minutes is short enough to maintain focus
  • The timer creates urgency
  • Regular breaks prevent burnout
  • It makes large revision tasks feel manageable

4. Interleaving

Instead of studying one topic for three hours straight (blocked practice), mix different topics within a single revision session. This feels harder — and that's the point. The difficulty of switching between topics strengthens your ability to distinguish between concepts and apply the right approach.

5. Past Papers

Past papers are the single most effective revision tool. They show you exactly what examiners are looking for, expose gaps in your knowledge, and build exam technique. Start using them early — not just the week before your exam.

How to use them effectively:

  1. Do the paper under timed conditions
  2. Mark it yourself using the mark scheme
  3. For every mark you dropped, understand exactly why
  4. Redo the same questions a week later to check you've actually learned from your mistakes

What doesn't work

For completeness, here are common methods that feel productive but have minimal impact on learning:

  • Re-reading notes: Passive and creates an illusion of familiarity without real understanding
  • Highlighting: Satisfying but doesn't engage your brain in meaningful processing
  • Copying notes out again: Unless you're actively transforming the information, this is just handwriting practice
  • Watching YouTube videos passively: Great for initial understanding, but you need active recall afterwards to retain it

Building your system

The best study system combines these techniques. A typical revision session might look like:

  1. Review your notes briefly to refresh the topic (5 mins)
  2. Close your notes and do active recall — write what you remember (10 mins)
  3. Check what you missed and focus on those gaps (5 mins)
  4. Do practice questions under timed conditions (25 mins)
  5. Mark and review — understand every mistake (10 mins)

Then schedule the same topic for review in a few days. Rinse and repeat.
