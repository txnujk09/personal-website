How to Choose Your A-Levels

6 min read

Choosing A-Levels feels like one of the biggest decisions you'll make — and it is important. But it's not as permanent as it feels. Here's a practical framework for making the right choice.

Start with what you're good at

This sounds obvious, but it's easy to overlook. A-Levels are harder than GCSEs, and the subjects where you have natural aptitude will be the ones where you can achieve the highest grades. High grades keep more doors open than "impressive" subject choices with mediocre results.

Think about what you might study at university

Some university courses have specific A-Level requirements. If you have any idea what you might want to study, check the entry requirements now — not in Year 13 when it's too late to change.

  • Medicine: Biology and Chemistry are essential at most universities
  • Engineering: Maths and Physics are usually required
  • Computer Science: Maths is almost always required; Further Maths is helpful for top unis
  • Economics: Maths is required or strongly preferred at most Russell Group universities
  • Law: No specific subjects required, but essay-based subjects help

Keep your options open

If you're not sure what you want to do yet — that's completely normal. Pick subjects that give you flexibility. Maths is the single most versatile A-Level: it's valued by almost every university course and employer. Combining Maths with one humanities subject and one science/social science gives you the broadest range of options.

Don't pick subjects just because they sound impressive

Further Maths, Physics, and Chemistry are respected subjects — but only if you can get the grades. Three A*s in subjects you enjoy will always beat three Bs in subjects you chose to look clever.

The best A-Level combination is one where you can achieve high grades, enjoy the content, and meet the entry requirements for courses you're interested in.

What about "facilitating subjects"?

Russell Group universities used to publish a list of "facilitating subjects" — the ones most commonly required for entry. While this list is no longer officially maintained, the principle still holds. Subjects like Maths, English, Sciences, History, Geography, and Languages are widely accepted and rarely restrict your options.

My recommendation

If you're still unsure, start with Maths (it's the most universally useful), add one subject you genuinely enjoy, and pick a third that complements the other two or opens up a specific pathway you're considering. Don't overthink it — what matters more than your subject choices is how well you do in them.
